Two small slips in the paper (so they don't confuse you): it calls the gap after a frame a 7-bit "IFS", but the standard's intermission is 3 bits. It also describes the CRC loosely as a count of bits. It's actually a 15-bit check value calculated from the frame's bits, which the receiver recalculates and compares.

2. The big picture

In the paper: §1 (p2), §2 Fig 1 (p2), §5 Fig 6 (p7)

CAN lets many small computers (nodes) share one pair of wires: motor controllers, sensors, your ESP32. Nobody is in charge. Any node can talk, and every node hears every message. A message carries a label (the identifier) saying what it is, such as "motor current" or "battery voltage", not who it's for. Each node decides for itself whether it cares. That's why cars, robots and drones use it: you can add a node without rewiring anything else.

Every node has two parts:

  • Controller: the brain. It builds and reads messages, checks for errors and handles arbitration. It works with ordinary logic signals on two pins: TX (out) and RX (in). On your board it's the TWAI peripheral inside the ESP32.
  • Transceiver: the translator. It converts the controller's TX logic into the two-wire bus voltages, and converts the bus back into RX logic. On your board it's the SN65HVD230 module.

Check yourself

  1. If you unplugged node 2, would nodes 1 and 3 still be able to talk? Why?

3. Wiring and termination

In the paper: §5 (p7), the paragraph after Fig 6 on the 120 Ω cable and termination

At CAN speeds (up to 1 Mbit/s) a long wire doesn't behave like a simple conductor. It behaves like a transmission line: each voltage step travels along the cable as a wave. When the wave hits an open end, part of it bounces back, like an echo in a corridor, and the echoes smear into the next bits.

Twisted-pair CAN cable has a characteristic impedance Z0 ≈ 120 Ω. That's the ratio of voltage to current the wave "sees" as it travels, set by the cable's geometry, not its length. If the end of the line is a resistor equal to Z0, the wave can't tell the cable has ended. Its energy goes into the resistor as heat and nothing reflects.

  • One 120 Ω resistor at each physical end, so there are exactly two on the whole bus.
  • Not one per node. The standard says not to put the terminator on a node, because unplugging that node would remove the termination.
  • Keep the stubs (the short branches from bus to node) short: the paper gives 0.3 m maximum. The ISO 11898 headline numbers are 1 Mbit/s, 40 m and 30 nodes.
  • The two resistors also do a DC job: when no transceiver is driving, they pull CAN-H and CAN-L to the same voltage, which is the recessive state.

Multimeter tip: with the power off, the meter across CAN-H and CAN-L sees every termination resistor on the bus at once. The transceivers are effectively open circuits when unpowered. Work out what a correctly terminated bus should read; that's Q2.

Check yourself

  1. What would the meter read if one terminator were missing? And with three (an extra one on a middle node)?

4. Voltages and noise: recessive and dominant

In the paper: §5 p7 (voltage levels), Fig 7 (p8), §5.1.4 Fig 11 (p11), §4.1 Fig 4 (p4) for the inverted logic

The bus has only two states:

StateLogicCAN-HCAN-LH − LWho causes it
Recessive1≈ 2.5 V≈ 2.5 V≈ 0 VNobody. It's the resting state the resistors pull to.
Dominant0≈ 3.5 V≈ 1.5 V≈ 2 VAny transceiver actively driving

These are the paper's numbers for 5 V transceivers. Your 3.3 V SN65HVD230 sits a bit lower, but the pattern and the roughly 2 V difference are the same. Note the "inverted" feel: logic 0 is the active state (paper Fig 4).

The receiver ignores the absolute voltages and looks only at the difference H − L. Interference from a motor, an ESC or a radio couples onto both wires of a twisted pair almost equally, because they run so close together and the twist swaps their positions every few millimetres. A disturbance that's the same on both wires is called common-mode, and subtraction cancels it. The paper adds a second benefit: equal and opposite currents in the two wires mean their magnetic fields mostly cancel, so the bus also emits very little noise. That's the EMC link to MBDA.

The rule that makes everything else work: dominant always beats recessive. If one node drives dominant while others leave the bus alone, the bus is dominant. A 0 overwrites a 1. Arbitration and ACK both rely on this.

Check yourself

  1. A 1 V spike lands on both wires during a dominant bit. What are CAN-H, CAN-L and H − L during the spike?
  2. Why would running CAN-H and CAN-L as two separate, untwisted wires a few centimetres apart make noise rejection worse?

5. What a message looks like: the standard data frame

6. Arbitration: two nodes talk at once

In the paper: §4.1 and Figs 4–5 (pp4–5). The paragraph about each node "monitoring its own transmission" is the key one.

There's no master to hand out turns. A node can start a frame whenever the bus is idle, so two nodes can start on exactly the same bit. CAN sorts this out during the identifier, bit by bit, using two facts:

  1. Dominant beats recessive (section 4).
  2. Every transmitter reads the bus back while it talks. The transceiver's receiver is always connected, so the controller compares the bit it sent with the bit it sees.

While both nodes send the same bits, nothing seems odd: the bus shows what both sent. At the first bit where they differ, one sends 0 (dominant) and the other sends 1 (recessive). The bus shows 0. The node that sent 1 reads back a 0, realises someone with a lower-numbered ID is talking, and stops transmitting and becomes a listener. The winner never notices anything happened.

This is called non-destructive bitwise arbitration. Read the paper's §4.1 for its definition of "non-destructive" and decide for yourself why the winner's frame isn't damaged (Q4). The loser retries automatically as soon as the bus is idle again.

Design consequence: the system designer chooses IDs so that urgent messages get low numbers. The paper's example gives motor winding current a lower ID than tachometer speed.

Check yourself

  1. Node C with ID 0x0FF joins the same contest. At which bit does it diverge from A, and who wins overall?

7. ACK and errors

In the paper: the ACK bullet under Fig 2 (p3), §4.3 (p6), §4.4 (pp6–7), §4.2.3 error frame (p6). ESP-IDF: Bus Errors and Recovery.

The ACK slot

The transmitter sends a recessive bit in the ACK slot and reads the bus back. Every other node that received the frame correctly (CRC matches) drives that bit dominant. So:

  • Bus reads dominant in the ACK slot → at least one node got the frame correctly.
  • Bus stays recessive → nobody acknowledged. The transmitter flags an ACK error and, like any CAN error, automatically retransmits the frame.

Note what ACK does not tell you: it doesn't say which node received it, or that the node you meant to reach did. It only says "somebody on the bus got this correctly".

The error counters

Every controller keeps two counters: the transmit error counter (TEC) and the receive error counter (REC). Errors push them up; successful frames slowly bring them down. The counters move the node between three states:

StateWhenBehaviour
Error-activeTEC and REC < 128Normal. Can send loud (dominant) error frames.
Error-passiveTEC or REC ≥ 128Still talks, but may only flag errors quietly (recessive). Can't disrupt others.
Bus-offTEC > 255Disconnected from the bus. Recovery needs a long run of recessive bits (ESP-IDF: Bus Errors and Recovery).

This is the paper's "fault confinement": a broken node can't hog the bus forever.

For Q5: picture a single ESP32 + transceiver on a bus with no other node. Use the ACK rule and the table above to predict what the transmitter experiences: what it sees in the ACK slot, what it does next, and what happens to its TEC over time. Write your prediction, then we'll test it with real hardware. There's one subtlety we'll check in the tutored session.

ESP32 modes worth knowing (ESP-IDF page):

  • Self-test mode: the controller doesn't require an ACK, so one node can transmit alone. Useful for a first bench test.
  • Listen-only mode: receives only and never drives a dominant bit, not even ACKs or error frames. This is the mode the project README requires on a car's CAN bus.

8. Bit stuffing (bonus question)

In the paper: the last paragraph of §4.4 (p6), plus the EOF bullet (p3)

CAN has no separate clock wire. Receivers keep their bit timing locked by watching for edges, the moments the bus changes between recessive and dominant. A long run of identical bits has no edges, so a receiver's clock could drift and miscount.

The rule: after 5 identical bits in a row, the transmitter inserts one bit of the opposite value. Receivers know the rule and delete the stuffed bit automatically, so your code never sees it.

Stuffing has two more payoffs. Six identical bits in a row can now only mean something deliberate: an error frame is six dominant bits on purpose, breaking the rule so every node notices. And seven recessive bits (EOF) can only mean "frame over". Stuffing applies from SOF to the end of the CRC sequence. The fixed-format tail (CRC delimiter, ACK, EOF) isn't stuffed.

Check yourself

  1. Stuff this sequence: 1 1 1 1 1 1 1 1 0. How many bits go on the wire?

9. Your ESP32 hardware

In ESP-IDF: Overview; Getting Started → Hardware Connection. In the paper: §5 first paragraph and Fig 6 (p7). It even uses the SN65HVD230 as its example transceiver.

  • The ESP32 contains a CAN controller (TWAI) but no transceiver. The ESP-IDF page states this explicitly, which is why you bought the SN65HVD230 module.
  • The controller talks to the transceiver through two logic signals: TWAI TX (ESP32 → transceiver driver input, often labelled CTX or D on modules) and TWAI RX (transceiver receiver output → ESP32, labelled CRX or R).
  • Which GPIOs carry TX and RX isn't fixed: you choose them in software when you configure the TWAI node. The ESP32 can route the signals to most GPIOs.
  • The module also needs 3V3 and GND from the ESP32, and its CANH/CANL pins go to the bus.
  • For a one-board test, ESP-IDF suggests connecting TX directly to RX (no transceiver) and using self-test mode.

Check your modules when they arrive: many cheap SN65HVD230 breakout boards already have a 120 Ω resistor soldered between CANH and CANL. With two such boards at the two ends of a short bus, that's exactly the right termination. Add a third board in the middle and you'd have an extra terminator. Measure with the borrowed multimeter (power off) and compare with your answer to Q2.

11. Glossary

NodeAny device on the bus (controller + transceiver + its microcontroller).
Recessive / dominantBus logic 1 (idle, nobody driving) / bus logic 0 (actively driven; always wins).
DifferentialInformation carried in the difference between two wires, not in either wire's voltage.
Common-modeAnything that's the same on both wires, such as noise or ground offset. A differential receiver rejects it.
Characteristic impedance Z0The V/I ratio a travelling wave sees on a cable; 120 Ω for CAN twisted pair.
TerminationA resistor equal to Z0 at each end to stop reflections.
StubThe short branch from the main bus to a node. Keep it ≤ 0.3 m.
Identifier (ID)11-bit label for what a message is; also its priority (lower = more urgent).
ArbitrationDeciding who transmits when several start together; bitwise and non-destructive in CAN.
CRCCyclic redundancy check: a 15-bit check value that detects corrupted bits.
ACKAcknowledge slot: receivers overwrite the transmitter's recessive bit with dominant.
TEC / RECTransmit / receive error counters; they set error-active, error-passive or bus-off.
Bit stuffingInserting an opposite bit after 5 identical ones so receivers stay in sync.
TWAIEspressif's name for the CAN controller inside the ESP32.
TransceiverChip converting logic TX/RX to bus CANH/CANL and back (here the SN65HVD230).
